How he writes questions

  • “As written” redox stems. A reaction, then who oxidizes whom, or which pair supplies the reductant. Pure vocabulary and very high yield.
  • Scenario stems. Infant in the cold, sitting to running, ATP/ADP of 90 vs 100, a mutation that slows Fo. Ask what changed first and which way each rate moves.
  • EXCEPT, NOT, LEAST, MOST directly. Read the stem twice.
  • One joke distractor. “Nervous,” “free energy rays,” “Gravity,” “Transmutate into a nitrogen atom.” Eliminate it.
  • Precision traps. Wrong carrier type, wrong compartment, wrong charge sign, or a textbook myth (FADH₂ released; “proton gradient”).
  • No calculator math. Expect sign, direction and ranking questions about ΔE°′ and ΔG°′ instead.

What past students missed

  • NAD⁺/NADH must accept or donate two electrons simultaneously (2025 student picked “one or two”).
  • The electron wire in Complex I’s matrix arm is Fe-S centers, not NADH (2025).
  • NADH cannot donate one electron directly to an Fe-S center (2024).
  • Δψ charges stay at the membrane because opposite charges attract (2024).

His pet peeves

  • Complex II is succinate dehydrogenase. FADH₂ is never released or bound as a substrate by any mitochondrial enzyme.
  • Mitochondria make a Δψ of ~180 mV, positive outside (IMS), negative inside (matrix), not a ΔpH. Boards will still say “proton gradient.”
  • CIII pumps 2 H⁺ per 2e⁻; CIV pumps 4.
  • Ubisemiquinone is never released from a UQ binding site.

Redox vocabulary and ET equations

Every ET reaction is two redox pairs trading electrons. The vocabulary is the most testable part of the section.

Definitions to say from memory

  • RAM (redox active molecule): a molecule that contains a RAMO.
  • RAMO (redox active molecular orbital): one specific outer-shell orbital that holds its 1 (sometimes 2) electron(s) less tightly than all other orbitals. Picture the electron flickering in and out; that weak hold is why it can be passed to another RAMO.
  • Non-redox active molecule: every outer electron tightly bound; releasing one would take extraordinary energy (very high heat).
  • Reduced: the RAMO holds its electron(s). Oxidized: the RAMO is empty.
  • Redox pair (couple): the oxidized and reduced forms that share the same RAMO (fumarate/succinate, NAD⁺/NADH).
  • Aox and Ared are chemically distinct molecules, because chemistry is set by the number and distribution of outer-shell electrons.
  • Convention: pairs are listed oxidized/reduced, or as a half-reaction Aox + e⁻ → Ared.
  • Food-electron bookkeeping: the high-energy electrons of food hydrocarbons are those associated with H; they leave the body on H₂O.

  • ET can be one part of a larger enzyme reaction (coupled to phosphorylation or decarboxylation); the ET part still fits Equation 1.
  • Almost all cellular ET moves 1 or 2 electrons at a time.

The five-second method for “as written” stems

  1. Find the reactant that gains electrons or H (NAD⁺→NADH, UQ→UQH₂, DHAP→glycerol 3-P). That is the oxidant; it gets reduced.
  2. The other reactant loses electrons: it is the reductant; it gets oxidized.
  3. Phrase it as “[reductant] reduces [oxidant] to [its reduced form]” or “[oxidant] oxidizes [reductant] to [its oxidized form].”
  4. The pair supplying the reductant is written oxidized/reduced of the reductant (Pyruvate/Lactate).

Ubiquinone (topic 11)

  • Restricted to the IMM by its 50-carbon isoprenoid tail: lipid-soluble, not water-soluble. UQ and UQH₂ both diffuse freely in the bilayer.
  • Usually only the headgroup enters a binding site. Complex I is the exception: a tunnel admits the whole extended molecule (~30 Å).
  • Six of seven IMM ET complexes bind UQ/UQH₂. Five reduce UQ (CI, CII, dihydroorotate DH, mitochondrial glycerol 3-P DH, ETF-UQ oxidoreductase). Only CIII oxidizes UQH₂. Only CIV never touches UQ.

Semiquinones and superoxide (topic 14)

  • Only semiquinones (ubisemiquinone, flavin semiquinone) reduce O₂ to superoxide. Fully oxidized and fully reduced forms do not.
  • Superoxide → ROS → oxidative damage to lipids and proteins (oxidative stress).
  • Flavins are protected by permanent burial in protein.
  • UQ sites let UQ and UQH₂ come and go but block release of ubisemiquinone.

Complex II as the model ET complex

Succinate (matrix, water-soluble) and UQ (IMM, lipid-soluble) can’t share one active site, so the enzyme has two binding sites joined by an electron wire.

  1. Succinate binds near covalent FAD (subunit 1), donates 2e⁻ + 2H⁺ → FADH₂ + fumarate (released to matrix).
  2. FADH₂ gives 1e⁻ to Fe-S 1 (leaving FAD semiquinone), then the second once Fe-S 1 empties. Wire: [2Fe-2S] → [4Fe-4S] → [3Fe-4S]. FAD’s protons return to the matrix.
  3. The UQ headgroup binds in subunit 3 just inside the IMM, takes 2e⁻ one at a time from Fe-S 3 and 2H⁺ from the matrix, and leaves as UQH₂.
Succinate → FAD → Fe-S → Fe-S → Fe-S → UQ → UQH₂ (released into IMM)

Textbooks that describe a soluble succinate dehydrogenase releasing FADH₂ for a separate Complex II are wrong. Complex II and succinate dehydrogenase are the same enzyme, and FADH₂ is never a released product.

Redox potentials and free energy

Electrons move from the more negative E°′ pair to the more positive one, but the cell’s concentration ratios decide which way a reaction actually runs.

What a redox potential means

  • Each pair’s RAMO has its own affinity for electrons. E°′ (V or mV) quantifies that affinity for one pair, not for a whole reaction.
  • More negative = weaker hold = better donor. More positive = tighter hold = better acceptor.
  • In any ET reaction, the more negative pair supplies the reductant and the more positive pair supplies the oxidant.
  • Measured at 50% oxidized / 50% reduced, pH 7, against 2H⁺/H₂. Same value per electron transferred. Cellular range about −600 to +820 mV.

Total ΔG: why reactions run uphill (topics 17–20)

ΔG = ΔG°′ + 1.36 log ([products]/[reactants])kcal/mol at 37 °C; each 10× change in the ratio is worth 1.36
  • Total ΔG, not ΔG°′, sets direction. Reactions run in the −ΔG direction.
  • ΔG°′ is a constant from the relative chemical stability of products and reactants.
  • The second term is the force of the steady-state ratio in that compartment. Low [products]/[reactants] pushes right.
  • Steady-state concentration of any metabolite is set by its rate of production versus its rate of consumption (topic 19).

Malate dehydrogenase, the classic example

  1. ΔG°′ = +6.9 kcal/mol as written, because OAA/malate (−170 mV) holds electrons more tightly than NAD⁺/NADH (−320 mV).
  2. Matrix NAD⁺/NADH ≈ 10 because oxphos oxidizes NADH faster than dehydrogenases make it. That helps by ~1.4 kcal/mol but is not enough alone.
  3. The principal factor is an extremely low matrix [OAA], pulled away by citrate synthase (acetyl-CoA keeps arriving), by transamination to aspartate, and into gluconeogenesis in liver and kidney.
  4. Malate is made continuously, so [malate] stays relatively high.
  5. Net ΔG < 0. OAA can drop so low that pyruvate carboxylase is needed to replenish it.

Topic 20, reversibility as flexibility. ΔG°′ is fixed but ratios differ, so one reaction can run forward in one compartment and backward in another. Malate DH makes malate in the cytoplasm and oxidizes it in the matrix (malate–aspartate shuttle). Lactate DH makes lactate in red cells and oxidizes it in liver, heart and brain.

ATP, nucleophilic chemistry and coupling

ATP drives unfavorable bond-forming steps only when its cleavage is built into the same enzyme mechanism.

Why ATP hydrolysis releases energy

  • ΔG°′ = −7.3 kcal/mol; in cells ≈ −11 kcal/mol because ATP/ADP is held near 100:1 or higher.
  • Reasons: relief of charge repulsion among phosphates and resonance stabilization of both products.
  • Cellular Pi ≈ 3–5 mM, higher than ATP or ADP.
  • GTP and UTP couple some reactions too; they are made from ATP (ATP + GDP ⇌ ADP + GTP).

How a nucleophile removes the terminal phosphate

  • The γ-phosphorus is electropositive (δ+) because its surrounding O atoms withdraw electrons.
  • A strong nucleophile with a lone pair, often a full negative charge, attacks that δ+ P.
  • ADP²⁻ leaves easily (relief of charge repulsion). This is phosphorylation of the nucleophile, a nucleophilic addition.

Glutamine synthetase: acyl phosphate chemistry

Topic 21 says you don’t need to memorize this chemistry; know the concept (phosphorylation makes the carbon easier for a nucleophile to attack). The three moves:

  1. Phosphorylation. Glutamate’s side-chain carboxyl O⁻ attacks the δ+ γ-P of ATP → γ-glutamyl phosphate (an acyl phosphate) + ADP.
  2. Electron withdrawal. The attached phosphate pulls electron density off the carbonyl C, making it more susceptible to attack by an electron pair.
  3. Substitution. NH₃ attacks the carbonyl C and Pi leaves (a good, resonance-stabilized leaving group). An SN2-type substitution gives glutamine.

Why it matters: glutamine is the main non-toxic carrier of NH₃ in blood.

The same idea in four of his phrasings

  • Phosphorylation of a bound intermediate promotes nucleophilic substitution at the C bonded to the phosphate.
  • The phosphate group performs electron withdrawal on the target C.
  • Phosphate on a carbonyl C makes the C more likely to form a bond with a nucleophile.
  • The P of the terminal phosphate is electropositive due to electron withdrawal by surrounding O atoms.

Coupling: the cogged wheels (topic 23)

  • An exergonic part and an endergonic part are mechanistically linked in one enzyme, so the exergonic part drives the endergonic part.
  • ATP hydrolysis must be inside the mechanism to shift ΔG. Hydrolyzing ATP elsewhere does nothing.
  • Neither part runs without the other. If the endergonic half slows, the exergonic half slows to the same extent.

How pumping makes Δψ (Δψ items 1–8)

  1. Exergonic ET through CI, CIII and CIV drives electrogenic H⁺ pumping from the matrix to the IMS.
  2. Stoichiometry per 2e⁻: CI 4, CIII 2, CIV 4.
  3. Each pumping event is a charge separation: the H⁺ leaves, its partner anion stays in the matrix. Opposite charges attract across the membrane, so positive charge lines the IMS surface and negative charge lines the matrix surface. They don’t diffuse away because they attract each other.
  4. The attraction creates an electrical field or potential, Δψ (volts). Its magnitude depends on 1) the number of charges on each side and 2) the distance between them. By Coulomb’s law, force rises as distance falls; membranes are thin (~40 Å), so relatively few charge separations make a large Δψ.
  5. Δψ is also called mitochondrial membrane potential or PMF.
  6. Average 180 mV, positive outside, negative inside.
  7. Δψ only affects ion movements within the membrane. It has no effect on neutral processes in the membrane and no significant effect on molecules or solvent in the compartments on either side.
  8. Board questions will call it a “proton gradient.” Not accurate, but traditional.

Why not ΔpH (slides 30–31)

A ΔpH of 3 (worth 180 mV) would need a cytoplasmic pH near 4, and buffering would demand roughly 300,000 times more pumping events. Electrogenic pumps make Δψ; electroneutral pumps make ΔpH.

Respiratory control and uncoupling

ADP availability sets the speed of the whole H⁺ circuit while Δψ barely moves. UCP-1 collapses Δψ to zero, so ATP synthesis stops and ET runs as fast as NADH supply allows.

The rules of coupling (items 1–4)

  1. Both coupled processes must happen at the same time. If one speeds, slows or stops, the other does the same.
  2. The more exergonic process drives the more endergonic one.
  • H⁺ flow through ATP synthase is coupled to ADP + Pi → ATP. It needs H⁺ flow and ADP + Pi binding. Even with Δψ present, the β sites can’t cycle if ADP and Pi don’t bind, so γ rotation, c-ring rotation and H⁺ flow through Fo all require both PMF and ADP + Pi binding.
  • ET through CI, CIII and CIV is coupled to their H⁺ pumping.
  • Cellular ATP + ADP is constant, and both are transported across the IMM. So falling ATP/ADP means rising matrix [ADP]. Pi is normally high enough.

Consequences (items 7–8)

  • Faster oxphos → faster NADH oxidation → more NAD⁺ → the NAD⁺-dependent dehydrogenases of glycolysis (GAPDH), PDH, the TCA cycle and fatty acid oxidation run faster, if their food substrate is available. The reverse happens as ATP/ADP rises. Oxphos sets the pace of upstream catabolism.

UCP-1 in brown fat (UCP-1 items 1–7)

  1. UCP-1 is an integral membrane protein found only in the IMM of brown fat mitochondria.
  2. Activation chain: cold temperature → norepinephrine → fatty acids. Each active UCP-1 opens a path for rapid H⁺ transfer across the IMM, and Δψ goes to zero.
  3. With no Δψ, ATP synthase activity goes to zero.
  4. With no Δψ there is no electrostatic backpressure, so pumping speeds up, and because pumping is coupled to ET, ET from NADH to O₂ speeds up to the maximum rate allowed by the NADH supply.
  5. Uncontrolled ET and pumping generate heat.
  6. Infants: brown fat sits adjacent to the heart, lungs and kidneys and heats them when the infant is cold, because infants don’t shiver.
  7. Brown fat persists in adults; the role of UCP-1 in adults is still under investigation.

Glucose, fructose and galactose catabolism

Sugars are absorbed by Na⁺-driven and passive transport, trapped by phosphorylation, and split by glycolysis into 2 pyruvate, 2 net ATP and 2 NADH that must be re-oxidized for glycolysis to continue.

Digestion and uptake (items 1–7)

  • (1) Digestion breaks starch, lactose and sucrose down to glucose, galactose and fructose in the lumen of the small intestine. Glycosidases act in saliva (starch hydrolysis starts in the mouth), stomach, pancreas and intestine. Cellulose stays intact and leaves as fiber.
  • (2) Glucose and galactose cross the intestinal epithelium in three steps:
    • SGLT1 (lumen side) co-transports glucose or galactose with Na⁺. This is secondary active transport; the cell must keep low internal Na⁺. SGLT1 hydrolyzes no ATP.
    • The Na⁺/K⁺ pump (blood side) uses ATP to pump Na⁺ out, keeping the gradient.
    • GLUT-2 (blood side) lets glucose and galactose leave down their gradient into blood.
  • Fructose enters from the lumen by its own uniporter, not SGLT1.

(3) Oral rehydration therapy

Secretory diarrhea (e.g., cholera) kills by dehydration. Drinking dilute glucose + NaCl works because SGLT1 takes up glucose and Na⁺ together, and GLUT-2 plus the Na⁺/K⁺ pump move them into blood. Both solutes must stay dissolved, so water follows them from lumen to blood.

  • (4) GLUTs are facilitated-diffusion uniporters. Phosphorylation to glucose 6-phosphate traps glucose because GLUTs don’t bind G-6-P.

Two ways to regulate an enzyme

  • Reversible phosphorylation: kinases add phosphate to surface Ser/Thr –OH; phosphatases remove it. Usually changes the active site’s affinity for substrate.
  • Allosteric regulation: a molecule binds a distant site and raises (activates) or lowers (inhibits) active-site affinity for substrate. One enzyme can have several allosteric sites.

Fatty acid oxidation and ethanol catabolism

β-oxidation in the matrix removes two carbons per cycle as acetyl-CoA, sending one NADH to Complex I and one pair of electrons through ETF to UQ.

Where and why

  • FA oxidation is the major source of energy for ATP synthesis in humans. It occurs in the mitochondrial matrix.
  • Active in muscle, heart, kidney cortex and liver; argued not to occur in brain.
  • Two-carbon fragments come off the carboxyl end as acetyl-CoA, which the TCA cycle can oxidize.

Activation and entry

  1. Fatty acyl-CoA synthetase couples the FA to CoA (ATP → AMP + PPi). For LCFA (14–20 C) this is in the cytoplasm and is the first thing that happens after uptake; for MCFA (8–12 C) it is in the matrix. CoA provides energy for later steps and makes the FA more water-soluble.
  2. CPT I (outer membrane, IMS side) swaps CoA for carnitine → fatty acyl-carnitine in the IMS.
  3. Translocase carries acyl-carnitine across the IMM in exchange for free carnitine.
  4. CPT II (matrix) swaps carnitine back for CoA → fatty acyl-CoA in the matrix.

Malonyl-CoA, an intermediate of FA synthesis, inhibits CPT I, so newly made fatty acids aren’t immediately oxidized (no futile cycle). Some diseases lower CPT I activity; carnitine supplements can help.

Gluconeogenesis

Liver (90%) and renal cortex (10%) rebuild glucose from lactate, amino acids and glycerol by reversing glycolysis, using four enzymes to get around its three exergonic steps, powered by fatty acid oxidation.

Why and where

  • Keeps blood glucose up in the fasting and extended fasting states; glucose is essential fuel for brain and erythrocytes.

Dietary fat, lipoproteins and fatty acid synthesis

In the fed state, dietary fat travels from gut to tissues in chylomicrons, excess fuel in liver becomes palmitate and TAG that leaves in VLDL, and lipoprotein lipase hands the fatty acids to muscle, heart and adipose.

Ingested lipids and cholesterol

  • We eat mainly triacylglycerol (TAG), plus phospholipids and cholesterol.
  • Humans don’t catabolize cholesterol for energy. It goes into membranes, bile salts and steroid hormones, or is excreted, much of it in feces as bile salts.
  • We also make cholesterol from acetyl-CoA; statins inhibit that synthesis to lower blood cholesterol.

Digestion and chylomicrons

  1. Fat forms globules in the small intestine.
  2. The liver adds bile salts (detergents such as cholate, made by hydroxylating cholesterol). They emulsify globules into small droplets; they do not hydrolyze anything.
  3. Pancreatic lipases turn each TAG into 2 fatty acids + 1 monoacylglycerol.
  4. Inside intestinal epithelial cells, acyl transferases rebuild TAG starting from monoacylglycerol.
  5. TAG and cholesterol esters are packaged into chylomicrons and released (via lymph) into the bloodstream.

A chylomicron is mostly TAG inside a monolayer of membrane lipid, with recognition proteins (apoproteins) in that monolayer.

Lipolysis and ketone bodies

In the fasting state, adipocytes release fatty acids (carried on serum albumin) and glycerol; the liver oxidizes the fatty acids and turns the excess acetyl-CoA into ketone bodies for other tissues, including the starving brain.

Lipolysis in white adipose

  • Adipocytes store TAG in one large lipid droplet. In the fasting state, cytoplasmic lipases “nibble away” at its edges: lipolysis.
  • Perilipins coat the droplet and protect it from lipases.
  • Epinephrine binds its receptor → protein kinase A phosphorylates perilipins → they move and open gaps → lipases reach the TAG.
  • Insulin strongly inhibits lipolysis in the fed state by keeping perilipin protection in place.

Where the products go

  • Free fatty acids are released to blood, bound by serum albumin, and delivered to tissues that oxidize them.
  • Glycerol travels free in blood and is taken up by several cell types, but mostly by the liver (gluconeogenesis).
  • Fatty acids not used for β-oxidation are re-esterified to glycerol in the liver and sent out again in VLDL.

Ketone bodies: a circulating form of acetyl-CoA

  • Made in liver mitochondria from acetyl-CoA. Acetyl-CoA stays inside cells; ketone bodies circulate.
  • Each has 4 carbons = two acetyl-CoA. Most tissues import them and turn them back into acetyl-CoA; liver cannot.
  • Made in the fasting state (and in long exercise) because fatty acid oxidation raises acetyl-CoA while OAA is withdrawn into gluconeogenesis, slowing the TCA cycle.

The logic of ketone bodies

  • A soluble, transportable form of acetyl-CoA that doesn’t require the liver to release its CoA.
  • In starvation the brain runs on ~50% ketone bodies, ~50% glucose. That lowers its glucose demand and slows the breakdown of muscle protein for gluconeogenesis.
  • Over days of fasting, blood β-HB rises far more than acetoacetate, while glucose and fatty acids stay fairly flat (slide 37).

Diabetic ketoacidosis

With insulin deficiency:

  1. Fatty acid release from adipocytes is continuously activated (HSL unchecked), flooding the liver with fatty acids.
  2. Gluconeogenesis is activated, depleting OAA.
  3. Ketone body synthesis runs out of control. Ketone bodies are acids; untreated, DKA can drop blood pH lethally.

Insulin and glucagon flip the liver between storing and releasing glucose by phosphorylating key enzymes and by setting the level of fructose 2,6-bisphosphate; AMP, glucose, G-6-P and Ca²⁺ fine-tune the same enzymes locally.

Two tools do the work: reversible phosphorylation (hormonal) and allosteric regulation (local).

Tool 1: reversible phosphorylation

  • Protein kinases phosphorylate surface –OH groups on serine and threonine; protein phosphatases remove them.
  • Phosphorylation activates some enzymes and inactivates others, usually by raising or lowering the active site’s affinity for substrate.

Glycogen synthase vs glycogen phosphorylase: opposite responses

Insulin raises glycogen synthase and lowers glycogen phosphorylase. Glucagon and epinephrine do the opposite.

Tool 2: allosteric regulation

  • Allosteric regulators bind a site distant from the active site and raise (activate) or lower (inhibit) the active site’s affinity for substrate.
  • One enzyme can have binding sites for several allosteric regulators.

Fructose 2,6-bisphosphate: the hormonal switch for glycolysis vs gluconeogenesis

  • F-2,6-BP is an allosteric regulator, not a pathway intermediate. (Don’t confuse it with F-1,6-BP, the product of PFK-1.)
  • Bound to PFK-1, it raises PFK-1’s affinity for fructose 6-phosphate and lowers its affinity for the allosteric inhibitors ATP and citrate → glycolysis ↑.
  • At the same time it inhibits fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase → gluconeogenesis ↓.

Making and destroying F-2,6-BP: the bifunctional enzyme

  • PFK-2 uses ATP to phosphorylate C2 of F-6-P → F-2,6-BP. FBP-2 (fructose bisphosphate phosphatase-2) removes it → F-6-P.
  • Both activities sit on one polypeptide (a dimer), the bifunctional enzyme, and are reciprocally regulated by phosphorylation.

Pentose phosphate pathway

The PPP oxidizes glucose 6-phosphate to make NADPH and ribose 5-phosphate; demand for NADPH turns on its oxidative branch, and demand for ribose 5-P sets the direction of its non-oxidative branch.

Why the PPP exists

  • Produces NADPH and ribose 5-phosphate, entirely in the cytoplasm. Most active in liver; makes ~60% of the body’s NADPH.
  • Cells with mitochondria keep cytosolic NAD⁺ oxidized (NAD⁺/NADH 10–700) but NADP⁺ highly reduced (NADPH/NADP⁺ ~10). NAD⁺ accepts electrons in catabolism; NADPH donates them in anabolism.

Oxidative branch

G-6-P —G-6-P dehydrogenase (NADP⁺ → NADPH)→ 6-phosphoglucono-δ-lactone —gluconolactonase (H₂O)→ 6-phosphogluconate —6-phosphogluconate DH (NADP⁺ → NADPH, CO₂)→ ribulose 5-P
  • Per G-6-P: 2 NADPH + 1 CO₂ + ribulose 5-P (→ ribose 5-P).
  • Glucose 6-phosphate dehydrogenase starts the branch and is set by NADPH demand:
    • Allosterically activated by NADP⁺.
    • Inhibited by NADPH (allosteric or product inhibition).
    So the NADPH/NADP⁺ ratio decides whether G-6-P is bound by G6PD.

Non-oxidative branch

  • Reversible “dance of carbon”: transketolases move 2-C pieces, transaldolases move 3-C pieces between sugars.
  • No NADPH is made here.

Old-exam answer, four years running: when a cell has plenty of NADPH but needs ribose 5-P, it pulls fructose 6-phosphate and glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate from glycolysis and reverses the non-oxidative reactions. When ribose 5-P is plentiful but NADPH is needed, G6PD is activated.
